# Sample Script 002 — "You Were Never the Word"

*Draft for tone/style validation only — no pipeline built yet. Rewrite of sample_script_001, aiming shorter and sharper.*

---

You open the app.

You close it two minutes later.

You tell yourself: tomorrow.

You've said that for a year.

Maybe longer.

And here's the part that stings — it's not the grammar.

It's not the verb tables. It's not the accent. It's not even the words.

You know more words than you think you do. You've known them for months.

You just won't say them out loud.

Because somewhere, a long time ago, you decided that being wrong in front of someone was worse than staying silent forever.

So you wait. One more app. One more course. One more "when I'm ready."

You will never be ready. Ready isn't a place you arrive at. It's a lie you tell so you don't have to speak today.

Let me tell you about a man who knew this better than anyone who has ever lived.

Nelson Mandela. Robben Island. A cell the size of a parking space. A life sentence with no release date.

The men guarding him spoke Afrikaans. The language of the system that put him there. The language of every order, every insult, every locked door.

He could have hated that language forever. Most men would have.

Instead — he learned it.

In prison. With no reason to. With nothing to gain that anyone could see.

His own people thought he'd lost his mind. Why learn the language of the men who hate you?

Because Mandela understood something almost nobody else does:

A language is not the property of the people who speak it. It's a door. And he wasn't going to let anyone keep him out of a single door — not even one built by his enemies.

He started speaking to the guards. Small sentences. Their language. Their words.

And something in those men cracked open. They stopped seeing a prisoner. They started seeing a man.

Mandela said it himself:

*"If you talk to a man in a language he understands, that goes to his head. If you talk to him in his language, that goes to his heart."*

He didn't say that about vocabulary. He said it about reaching people. About being felt, not just understood.

A man in a six-by-eight cell learned the language of his own captors — and you are sitting there with an app, a phone, and a hundred native speakers one click away, telling yourself you're not ready.

Mandela didn't have your freedom. You don't have his excuse.

This was never about the language.

It's about who you decide to be the next time you open your mouth and the wrong word comes out.

Say it wrong today. On purpose, if you have to.

Let someone hear you before you feel ready.

The wall was never the language.

It was you.

And you get to tear it down today — no permission required.
